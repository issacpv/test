# Does Triage Documentation Mediate Emergency-Care Disparities for Non-English-Preferring Patients? A MIMIC-IV-ED Study Using Granular Preferred-Language Groups

**Background.** Patients with limited English proficiency have more unplanned ED revisits, different admission patterns and longer delays, and Emergency Severity Index (ESI) triage depends on a brief history that a language barrier degrades. The mechanism usually invoked, that less information at triage leads to mis-triage and delay, has never been measured on open data. Existing MIMIC-IV-ED work used a binary English/non-English flag and reported associations only; single-centre studies report ESI-adjusted odds without documentation measures. MIMIC-IV v3 now records a standardised primary language.

**Objective.** We will test whether non-English-preferring (NEP) patients receive different ED processes and outcomes across granular language groups, whether chief-complaint informativeness mediates those differences, whether the ESI under-triages NEP patients at the same acuity, whether effects persist within ethnicity and shrink when an interpreter is documented, and whether triage ML models inherit the gap.

**Methods.** From MIMIC-IV-ED v2.2 (about 425,000 stays, 205,000 patients) linked to MIMIC-IV v3.1 and MIMIC-IV-Note, we will build a visit table with modal preferred language per subject (English, Spanish, Portuguese, Chinese languages, Russian, Haitian Creole, Vietnamese, Arabic, Other, Unknown), outcomes (admission, ED length of stay, time to first medication, 72-h return, critical outcome defined as ICU transfer within 12 h or in-hospital death), chief-complaint features (token count, complaint count, vagueness, barrier markers, missing pain score) and a local regex for interpreter mentions in discharge notes. We will fit adjusted logistic and quantile regressions with cluster-robust errors by subject, critical-outcome rates within ESI level with Wilson intervals and ESI AUROC per group, exact matching on ESI, sex, age band, arrival mode, race and insurance, and natural direct and indirect effects with 1,000 cluster-bootstrap resamples. Benchmark triage models retrained with patient-level splits and without language as a feature will be compared on sensitivity at a 10% alert rate and calibration per group. Three primary hypotheses use Holm correction; triage temperature is the negative-control outcome; selection into the language-known cohort is handled by inverse-probability weighting.

**Expected results.** We expect higher adjusted admission odds at ESI 3-5, longer stays and later first analgesia for NEP visits; shorter and vaguer complaints, largest for languages other than Spanish; a critical-outcome risk ratio above 1.2 at ESI 3 and ESI AUROC lower by at least 0.02; informativeness mediating at least 20% of under-triage; persistence within Hispanic/Latino patients; smaller gaps with documented interpreters; and lower model sensitivity for NEP visits that widens with text features.

**Significance.** Quantifying a documentation pathway to under-triage points to interpreter access and triage prompts as interventions and shows what deployed triage models inherit.
